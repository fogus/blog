Mind the van Emden Gap

Fogus

2026.04.21

I recently read an interesting paper written in 1982 by M.H. van Emden titled “Mental Ergonomics as Basis for New-Generation Computer Systems.” In the paper, van Emden described a “conversational partner” as a way to deal with the problem of software complexity at the time. He was also looking for a “congenial tool” for thought-workers that he called “Computer-Aided Thought” (CAT). He viewed his ideal conversational partner as a projection of his own reasoning that could react, critique, and extend his ideas as an interlocutor. He also saw the ideal CAT interlocutor as one that provided a source of productive friction by pushing back on his ideas to help stimulate more careful thinking. He didn’t expect that this conversational partner to have any true knowledge, but instead valued the reflexive nature of the tool in response to the valuable act of deliberately structuring his thoughts in such a way as to stimulate the tool. The process of structuring his thoughts in such a way to provoke the tool took a systematic form where each idea was broken into smaller sub-thoughts (so to speak), each precisely stated and each following from the last. Once he fed this into the CAT system, he hoped to observe how the system responded (or not) to his “argument” (for lack of a better term). When the system pushed-back he could revise the structure and contents of his argument to drive towards deeper understanding. He thought that these arguments should be written in a formal representation that enforced explicitness and logical support.1 He preferred a kind of logical language over English2 because it was more formal and necessarily constrained in ways that English isn’t. He saw the fluidity and ambiguity of English as a circumvention of the very process of formalizing one’s thoughts, something that van Emden viewed as critical to intellectual growth, and thus the necessary driving force behind how CAT systems should have been built.

But here we are in 2026, and a conversational language interface to so-called intelligent systems is the primary mode of interaction. While van Emden’s critiques of English interfaces were somewhat speculative3, we can now look at how people actually interact with modern LLM systems today. First, the inherent ambiguity in English is preserved and propagated during LLM interactions and response paths are silently chosen based on opaque interpretations by the systems unless explicitly prompted to do otherwise. An unprepared4 LLM is happy to generate plausible responses given any amount of ambiguity and rarely do they push back from clarification on their own. Second, hidden assumptions stay hidden, and the model just fills them in based on the opaque interactions of training weights in the system. In van Emden’s logic-based encoding, hidden premises are error conditions, requiring the user to tease them out and encode them explicitly. Third, even well-written prose can be problematic since it leads us to think that we’ve already thought. There’s a difference between rhetorical and logical and while the former can be complete, the latter can be shallow. As a result, we can easily trick ourselves into buying into an LLMs responses if they appear to cohere with our input. Fourth, LLMs still cannot show us its reasoning in a way that we can reliably verify. This might improve, but it’s unclear if market forces will drive the technology toward this goal. Fifth, LLMs do not naturally give useful error feedback5 and instead present all responses as equally plausible and with absolute confidence. It takes a lot of effort to coax an LLM into satisfying constraints rather than just producing likely continuations. This is antithetical to the “friction” that van Emden saw as necessary in CAT systems. Sixth, as context erodes, LLMs provide a shockingly fraught capability for “querying” the state of a discussion. The ideal for van Emden was that the argument at any moment should be queriable. And finally, how my words get turned into whatever the model actually does is completely hidden and only derivable with considerable effort (if at all) after the response has already been generated.

If you read my previous post on LLMs then you could guess that I’m much more in the van Emden camp regarding the kind of system that I wish to use for my own intellectual pursuits. However, the most fruitful discussions that I’ve ever had have been with other humans, using ambiguous language to great effect. So perhaps there is something to be said for a familiar conversational interface. Perhaps, but I fear that we’re not quite there with the current wave of LLM technologies. Rather than actually helping us to think better, today’s systems actively work to terminate deep thought and provide a kind of “mirror trap” for their users. I’ll talk about that trap another day, but for now I’ll say that it doesn’t have to be this way. Sadly, there currently appears to be no motivation by the LLM companies to move toward intelligence augmentation (IA) rather than intelligence replacement. That said, perhaps we can find a way to use these tools for intellectual betterment. It’s something that I’ve been thinking a lot about, but they won’t do it on their own. Instead, to echo van Emden, we need more systematic ways of interacting with these beasts to elevate, rather then echo, our thinking.
